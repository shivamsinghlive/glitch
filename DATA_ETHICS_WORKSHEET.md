## 1. Responsible Data Sourcing & Use
| Question | Team notes / actions taken |
|---|---|
| **Sources:** What data did you use? Who collected them, when, and for what purpose? | **TSHS Hot Flashes dataset**: 375 women aged 35–48, baseline of a 2001 cohort study (Freeman et al.), published by Grau, Okungu and Sammel (2023) for teaching. Used to train an educational hot-flash risk indicator. **NHANES 2017–18** (CDC National Center for Health Statistics): 1,811 US women aged 35–79, survey-weighted, collected for national health surveillance. Used for "you vs US women" percentiles. **Simulated nights and cycles** we generated with known answers, only to stress-test our detectors. **WHOOP-style demo data** is simulated, because no public WHOOP dataset exists. |
| **Quality:** How did you check accuracy, completeness, timeliness, relevance and gaps? | Cross-validated the model (5-fold, repeated 20 times, bootstrap interval AUC 0.73, range 0.64–0.76) and checked calibration against a baseline. Dropped rows with missing values. Used survey weights for NHANES and the standard rule for blood-pressure readings. Gaps we state openly: TSHS is a small 2001 cohort of 35–48-year-olds, so it can't speak for older or non-US women; the lower-education subgroup is 39 women; clinic blood pressure differs from home readings. Evidence passages behind the plans were checked against their source pages where we could open them (CDC, AAOS, Frontiers review, STRAW+10). The others come from the team's earlier research deck and a published-consensus summary, and should be re-checked against the originals before any clinical use. |
| **Permissions & attribution:** Are you permitted to access, reuse and share the data and related materials? How did you credit sources? | TSHS is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 for educational use; we use it only that way and label the output "educational, not a prediction for one person". NHANES is US government public domain. Raw datasets are never committed (git-ignored); only fitted coefficients and aggregate statistics are in the repo. The restricted mcPHASES dataset needs a signed data use agreement, so we did not use it. Sources are credited in the app (model card, plan evidence with links), README, ETHICS.md and the slides. |

## 2. Privacy, Bias & Fairness
| Question | Team notes / actions taken |
|---|---|
| **Privacy:** Do data contain personal or sensitive information? How did you minimize collection and protect identities? | Yes: menopause, cycle, blood-pressure, mood and optional hormone data are sensitive, and reproductive data is a known risk (FTC action against Flo Health, 2021). Everything is encrypted on the device (AES-GCM, non-extractable key); there is no backend database; the server logs nothing but its start-up line. Camera and microphone are analysed in the browser. The AI coach is the only thing that leaves the device, and the user chooses: **AI off**, **Chat only (default)**, or an opt-in **health summary** with the list of what is shared. In chat-only mode, each food photo needs consent. A live panel shows exactly what was sent. We audited our own first claim ("only text and a mood label"), found it wrong, and fixed it; tests prove chat-only mode leaks none of name, conditions, meals, stage or hormone data. Export and delete-everything are in the app. |
| **Bias & representation:** Who or what is missing or underrepresented? Could collection, labeling or analysis introduce bias? | Missing: women over 48, post-menopausal women, non-US women, anyone outside the 2001 cohort, people who can't afford a wearable. Possible sensor bias across skin tones, untested. We simulated it: a constant sensor offset has no effect (each woman is compared with her own baseline), but 3x noise cuts the night-sweat detector's precision from 94% to 78% and raises false alarms from 1% to 3.7%. Race and education are in TSHS; we **don't score people by them but use them to measure** differences. Regions can't be studied: neither dataset records where people live. |
| **Fairness:** Could results or decisions unfairly disadvantage, exclude or misrepresent people? What did you do to check? | We audited the model by race and education (leaving race out of a model doesn't make it fair; it only stops us measuring unfairness). Ranking (AUC 0.69 and 0.72 by race) and within-group calibration are similar, and adding race or education would not improve accuracy (AUC 0.72 vs 0.73). National NHANES data shows where real disparities are (age-standardised high blood pressure: Black women 62% vs White 38%). **But at one fixed cutoff, errors are not equal:** the model flags 54% of the African American group vs 30% of others, with false alarms of 38% vs 21%, because the groups' base rates differ (39% vs 25%). When base rates differ, calibration and equal error rates cannot both hold. We chose calibrated probabilities, show the gaps to users, and never use the flag to gate care or label a person. The lower-education result (39 women) is reported as a hint, not a verdict. |

## 3. Potential Harms & Mitigation
| Question | Team notes / actions taken |
|---|---|
| **Intended benefit:** Who is your project meant to help, and what positive outcome do you seek? | Women in perimenopause and menopause. About half of US women aged 40–60 had symptoms last year, only 15.6% take a prescription, and hot flashes last a median of 7.4 years. A 15-minute visit can't capture last month; Glitch turns voice notes and wearable data into a clear weekly summary for the doctor, and gives bone-and-muscle training and food targets built from the user's own choices. |
| **Potential harms:** Could misuse or unintended effects cause privacy violations, discrimination, misinformation, surveillance or other harm? Who is most affected? | Ten harms are tabled in ETHICS.md. Main ones: false reassurance (the night-sweat detector catches about 43% of real sweats); false alarms and anxiety; exposure of reproductive data, most serious where reproductive care is restricted; the AI giving wrong medical advice; stereotyping from ethnicity-aware messages; a model trained on a small old cohort; sensor bias across skin tones; mental-health screening misread as care; simulated data mistaken for real; people excluded by cost. |
| **Mitigation & limitations:** What safeguards have you implemented? What risks or limitations remain unresolved? | Safeguards: on-device encryption and no database; chat-only AI by default with per-photo consent and a live "what was sent" view; "no flag does not mean no sweat" message; alerts point to a clinician and the 988 line and never diagnose; the coach's actions are schema-validated, its health claims are grounded in a cited library, and the app works without the AI; the food photo analysis never invents numbers (it asks the user to type the meal in); every number labelled educational, estimated or simulated. Unresolved: no real wearable data or real night-sweat labels (detectors are stress-tested on simulation only), no testing with users, clinicians or across skin tones, small 2001 training cohort, wearable cost bias, and free-text AI errors. |

## 4. Transparency & Accountability
| Question | Team notes / actions taken |
|---|---|
| **Transparency:** Can users identify your data sources, methods and what your results mean? | Yes. A model card in the app lists data, accuracy, exclusions and limits. The *Model evidence* screen shows calibration, subgroup results, error-rate gaps, sensor stress tests and the "you vs US women" comparison. Each weekly plan shows the choices it was built from, why each exercise is there, and the cited passages it was grounded in (retrieved from a cited library, with links). Nutrition targets are computed with the published Mifflin-St Jeor equation, and the explanation shows the numbers. The README and slides separate real from simulated data. |
| **Communicating limitations:** How do you disclose uncertainty, risks and limits to prevent overconfidence? | Every estimate is labelled "educational", "estimated" or "simulated". The risk gauge compares with a study average instead of giving a diagnosis. The night-sweat card says it is "a guide, not a verdict" and that it catches under half of real sweats. Intervals and baselines are shown (AUC 0.73, range 0.64–0.76). The ethics document lists what we have not done. |
| **Accountability:** How can users report problems, and how would your team respond to errors, harm or feedback? | Honest gap: there is no in-app report button yet. Today, users can open an issue on the public repository, and every number is traceable to code and a test. We audited ourselves and fixed what we found (privacy claim, "equal across groups", accessibility). Next: an in-app "report a problem" link, a pilot with consent that includes a range of skin tones, and clinician review of the doctor summary. Users can export or delete all their data at any time. |

## Narrative version (150–250 words, for Devpost)

Glitch helps women in perimenopause and menopause turn wearable data and voice notes into evidence for their doctor. Data: we trained on the public TSHS Hot Flashes study (375 women, CC BY-NC-SA, used only for education) and used NHANES 2017–18 (CDC, public) for US reference ranges. We never commit raw data, and we label all wearable demo data as simulated because no public WHOOP data exists. Privacy: logs are encrypted on the device with no database, and the AI coach sees only what the user says by default. We found our first privacy claim was wrong, so we built three AI modes, per-photo consent and a live view of what was sent. Bias and fairness: we left race and education out of the model, then audited it. Ranking and calibration are similar across groups, but at one cutoff errors are not equal because base rates differ, so we publish the gaps and never use the flag to gate care. We stress-tested sensor bias: a constant offset is harmless, but noisy sensors cut precision from 94% to 78%. Harms: ten are tabled with mitigations, including "no flag is not reassurance". Transparency: a model card, an evidence screen, cited evidence behind every plan, and an accessibility audit with zero violations after fixes. Limits: simulated wearable data, a small 2001 cohort, and no real-user or skin-tone testing yet.
